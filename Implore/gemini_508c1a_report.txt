## Religious Entity Structuring: The 501(c)(3) vs. 508(c)(1)(A) Matrix
Establishing a religious entity to govern a software codebase like Implore requires navigating state-level corporate laws and federal tax regulations. Under Oklahoma law, the initial step in creating a nonprofit corporate shield is filing a Not for Profit Certificate of Incorporation, designated as SOS Form 0008, with the Oklahoma Secretary of State.
### State-Level Incorporation and Name Reservation
Under the Oklahoma General Corporation Act (Title 18, Section 1005), the Certificate of Incorporation must be executed by a minimum of three distinct incorporators. The corporate name must be distinguishable from other business entities registered in the state database and must include a specific designator such as "association," "company," "corporation," "foundation," "fund," "incorporated," "institute," "society," "limited," or their respective abbreviations.
To secure a specific name during the planning phase, an application for Reservation of Name (SOS Form 0034) can be submitted for a fee of $10.00, which holds the name for a non-renewable period of 60 days under 18 Okl. St. § 2009. The standard state filing fee for SOS Form 0008 is $25.00. Hand-delivered, same-day expedited filings incur an additional state fee of $25.00.
The physical street address of the church or principal office must be declared in the document, and post office boxes are strictly prohibited. Crucially, the standard boilerplate template provided by the Oklahoma Secretary of State does not satisfy the requirements of the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) for obtaining federal tax-exempt status. It must be manually amended to include explicit dissolution and operational limitation clauses that permanently dedicate corporate assets to an exempt purpose described in Section 501(c)(3).

### The 501(c)(3) vs. 508(c)(1)(A) Structural Comparison
The Internal Revenue Code contains a fundamental distinction between organizations that must notify the IRS of their claim to tax-exempt status and those that are automatically recognized as exempt. Section 501(c)(3) represents the standard statutory category for religious, charitable, and scientific organizations. To obtain formal IRS recognition, an organization must submit Form 1023 (with a $600.00 user fee) or Form 1023-EZ (with a $275.00 user fee).
To qualify for the streamlined Form 1023-EZ, the organization must project annual gross receipts under $50,000.00 and possess total assets valued below $250,000.00. Upon approval, the IRS issues a formal determination letter, which provides a definitive legal presumption of tax-exempt status.
Section 508(c)(1)(A) provides a mandatory exemption from the initial notification requirements of Section 508(a). This exemption dictates that "churches, their integrated auxiliaries, and conventions or associations of churches" are automatically recognized as tax-exempt Section 501(c)(3) organizations without being required to submit Form 1023 or Form 1023-EZ. While Section 508(c)(1)(A) provides an administrative exemption from the application process, it does not relieve the religious entity from complying with the substantive operational requirements of Section 501(c)(3).
The primary legal and operational differences between these regulatory paths are analyzed below:
| Legal Attribute | Formal 501(c)(3) Organization | Section 508(c)(1)(A) Church | Integrated Auxiliary of a Church |
|---|---|---|---|
| **IRS Filing Obligation** | Mandatory Form 1023 / 1023-EZ | Automatically exempt  | Automatically exempt  |
| **Form 990 Annual Return** | Mandatory unless explicitly exempt  | Automatically exempt under Sec. 6033  | Automatically exempt under Sec. 6033  |
| **Legal Presumption of Exemption** | Rebuttable presumption established | No presumption (*Branch Ministries*) | No independent presumption  |
| **Burden of Proof During Audit** | Borne by IRS; limited by church audit rules  | Borne entirely by entity or audited donor | Borne by entity to prove affiliation |

| **Commercial Contract Acceptance** | Universal acceptance by vendors and banks  | Frequently rejected without IRS letter  | Accepted via parent group exemption  |
### Legal Auditing, Tax Shelters, and the Burden of Proof
Relying solely on the automatic exemption of Section 508(c)(1)(A) without a formal IRS determination letter introduces severe legal risks, primarily centered on the reallocation of the burden of proof. Under established federal jurisprudence, most notably *Branch Ministries v. Rossotti*, an organization operating without an IRS determination letter has no legal presumption of tax exemption. In an audit, the burden of proof shifts to the organization or the individual donor to establish that the entity qualifies as a tax-exempt religious organization.
As established in *Jack Lane Taylor v. Commissioner*, if a donor is audited, the donor cannot rely on a presumption of deductibility; instead, the donor must legally prove that the church meets all the statutory and structural requirements of Section 501(c)(3). This requires demonstrating compliance with the strict IRS 14-point test.
Furthermore, "508 churches" and "corporations sole" are heavily scrutinized by the IRS due to their history of exploitation by tax shelter promoters. Promoters often market the corporation sole as a mechanism for individuals to declare themselves "bishops," take a "vow of poverty," and assign personal income to a one-person religious corporation to evade federal taxes, child support, and personal debts.
The Department of Justice and the IRS actively prosecute these schemes under 26 U.S.C. § 6700, penalizing promoters for making false or fraudulent statements regarding tax benefits. A legitimate corporation sole is designed only to ensure the continuity of property ownership for assets dedicated to a bona fide religious organization. Utilizing a Section 508(c)(1)(A) structure as a shell to shield licensing revenue or commercial profits derived from the Implore codebase will be classified as a sham transaction, exposing the organizers to civil and criminal liability.
### The 14-Point Test and the Definition of a Church
The term "church" is found but not specifically defined in the Internal Revenue Code. To analyze an entity's claim to church status, the IRS and the courts apply a 14-point test evaluating objective characteristics. These characteristics include a distinct legal existence, a recognized creed and form of worship, a definite and distinct ecclesiastical government, a formal code of doctrine and discipline, a distinct religious history, a membership not associated with any other church, an organization of ordained ministers selected after completing prescribed studies, a literature of its own, established places of worship, regular congregations, regular religious services, Sunday schools for religious instruction of the young, and schools for the preparation of ministers.
No single factor is controlling, but the presence of a regular congregation is considered an essential, high-weight requirement. In *American Guidance Foundation, Inc. v. United States*, the court ruled that at a minimum, a church must include a body of believers or communicants that assembles regularly to worship.
A technical group or development team maintaining the Implore codebase cannot satisfy this requirement. A congregation requires a physical, associational community. Praying in physical solitude or interacting purely through online forums, mail, or passive digital broadcasts does not constitute a "congregation" within the ordinary meaning of the word.
An alternative structure for technical religious operations is the "Integrated Auxiliary of a Church". Under Treasury Regulations, an integrated auxiliary is a separate legal entity that is described in Section 501(c)(3), is affiliated with a church or association of churches, and is internally supported. Affiliation must be structural, demonstrated by the church's authority to appoint or remove officers, common charter provisions, or formal group exemption coverage. An organization is internally supported if it does not offer goods, services, or facilities for sale to the general public on more than an incidental basis.
## Oklahoma County and Local Compliance Architectures
Operating a technical or religious entity in Oklahoma County requires adherence to state-level charitable registration laws, local municipal licensing, and specific tax compliance requirements governing business income.
### Charitable Solicitation and Local Business Licensing
Under Oklahoma law, any nonprofit organization that intends to solicit contributions from state residents must register with the Oklahoma Secretary of State by submitting Form 101-01/13. The initial registration fee is $15.00 for organizations expecting to receive $10,000.00 or less in annual contributions, or $65.00 for organizations exceeding that threshold.
Crucially, bona fide religious organizations are eligible for an exemption from this filing requirement. To secure the exemption, the entity must submit a formal letter to the Charitable Organizations Section of the Oklahoma Secretary of State outlining the specific religious basis for its exemption request.
At the municipal level, Oklahoma City and Oklahoma County do not enforce a general, statewide business license. However, municipal regulations mandate strict compliance with local zoning ordinances.
If the entity operates physical premises within Oklahoma County, it must secure Zoning Verification completed by city staff. Depending on the nature of the operations, physical facilities may also be subject to mandatory inspections conducted by the Oklahoma City-County Health Department (OCCHD) and the Oklahoma City Fire Department (OCFD).
If the entity hires employees, it must register with the Oklahoma Employment Security Commission (OESC) via the EZ Tax Express portal before executing its first payroll. For the year 2026, new employers are subject to a starting UI contribution rate of 1.5% on a taxable wage base of $25,000.00 per employee. Additionally, under Oklahoma law, workers' compensation insurance is mandatory starting with the first employee.
Under the McGirt judicial precedent (*McGirt v. Oklahoma*), standard state business regulations, licensing requirements, and tax obligations apply to all entities operating within the historic boundaries of an Indian reservation, unless the physical business is located on tribal trust land or restricted Indian land.
### Unrelated Business Income Tax (UBIT) and Form 990-T
A tax-exempt religious entity or church is still liable for taxes on its unrelated business income (UBI). Unrelated business income is defined as gross income derived from any trade or business that is regularly carried on and is not substantially related to the organization's tax-exempt purpose.
If a religious entity owns the Implore codebase and licenses it to commercial entities, charges subscription fees for its use, or operates it as a commercial Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) platform, this revenue constitutes UBIT. The IRS explicitly states that using commercial profits to fund legitimate religious activities does not shield that income from taxation; the "destination-of-income" test is rejected in favor of analyzing the nature of the activity itself.
The regulatory filing and payment requirements for UBI are structured as follows:
 * **Filing Threshold:** An exempt organization with gross income from an unrelated trade or business of $1,000.00 or more must file Form 990-T. This obligation is in addition to any other annual filing requirements.
 * **Tax Rates:** For corporate-structured nonprofits, UBI is taxable at federal corporate tax rates. For trusts, standard trust tax rates apply.
 * **Estimated Tax:** The organization must make quarterly estimated tax payments if its estimated tax on UBI is expected to be $500.00 or more for the year. Payments must be deposited electronically.
 * **Public Inspection:** Under IRC Section 501(c)(3), Form 990-T filings are subject to mandatory public inspection. The organization must make the return, including all supporting schedules, attachments, and financial details, available for public copying and inspection for three years from the filing due date.
There are narrow exceptions to UBI: activities conducted entirely by uncompensated volunteers, the sale of donated merchandise, or passive rental income from real property where at least 85% of the property is used directly for the organization's exempt purposes. However, commercial software licensing or the execution of automated SaaS transactions does not fall under these exceptions and remains fully taxable.

### Data Scraping Legality and CFAA Exposure
To bypass SAM.gov's rate limits or the administrative delays associated with official API key registration, developers sometimes integrate automated web scrapers or third-party scraping actors into their software architectures. This approach introduces severe legal liabilities and technical risks.
The SAM.gov Terms of Use explicitly prohibit the use of automated data gathering, web scraping tools, bots, or data mining software. If the GSA detects unauthorized scraping activities, it will terminate the associated Login.gov accounts and deny the offending IP addresses access to federal systems.
From a statutory standpoint, unauthorized scraping of federal portals exposes developers to liability under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA), codified at 18 U.S.C. § 1030. The CFAA imposes civil and criminal penalties on anyone who intentionally accesses a protected computer without authorization or exceeds authorized access.
The federal courts have established distinct parameters governing the legality of scraping:
 * **Public Data Protections:** In *hiQ Labs, Inc. v. LinkedIn Corp.*, the Ninth Circuit ruled that the CFAA does not apply to the web scraping of publicly available data that is not protected by authorization barriers (such as usernames and passwords).
 * **Revocation of Consent:** However, the courts have also held that if a platform explicitly revokes a scraper's permission to access its data, or if the scraper circumvents technological barriers (such as CAPTCHAs, rate limiting devices, or login gateways), subsequent scraping constitutes "access without authorization" under the CFAA.
 * **Contractual Breach:** Even if a CFAA claim is avoided, the target website can pursue claims for breach of contract, trespass to chattels, or copyright infringement if the scraper agreed to Terms of Service that explicitly forbid automated data extraction.
 * **GSA Best Practices:** The GSA’s Emerging Technology Office recommends that all entities engaging in data collection strictly adhere to the Robots Exclusion Protocol (robots.txt), identify their scrapers using transparent User-Agent strings, implement strict rate limiting, and respect any copyright control measures or digital rights management protections.
## Technical and Legal Liability Mitigation for the Implore Codebase
Deploying an interactive software system that processes federal procurement data and generates automated outputs introduces unique liabilities at the intersection of professional licensing, intellectual property, and product liability.
### Unauthorized Practice of Law (UPL) in Oklahoma
If the Implore codebase performs advanced operations—such as analyzing federal solicitations, drafting legal proposals, interpreting FAR regulations, or generating contract language—it operates within the domain of legal services. This creates substantial exposure to charges of the Unauthorized Practice of Law (UPL).
Under Title 5 of the Oklahoma Statutes and Rule 5.5 of the Oklahoma Rules of Professional Conduct, nonlawyers are strictly prohibited from practicing law or assisting others in doing so. The Oklahoma Supreme Court has declined to establish a rigid statutory definition of the "practice of law," opting instead for a functional test: the practice of law constitutes the rendition of any services requiring the knowledge and application of legal principles and techniques to serve the interests of another.
The Court has explicitly ruled that:
 * Drafting legal pleadings, agreements, or contracts constitutes the practice of law.
 * Negotiating settlements or legally binding commitments on behalf of third parties constitutes the practice of law.
 * Providing customized advice regarding the legal interpretation of regulations or statutes constitutes the practice of law.
If a non-attorney entity operates the Implore codebase to generate automated contract proposals, draft compliance documents, or provide regulatory advice directly to users, the entity and its developers can face civil injunctions, contempt proceedings, and criminal misdemeanor charges for UPL.
To mitigate this risk, the codebase must be structured strictly as a document-assembly and data-analytics tool rather than an automated legal advisor. It must function purely as a support mechanism under the direct, active supervision of a licensed attorney who reviews and approves all final outputs, thereby preserving compliance with professional standards.
### AI Hallucinations, Malpractice, and Product Liability
The deployment of generative AI algorithms introduces significant technical and operational risks, most notably "hallucinations"—the generation of fabricated, incorrect, or entirely fictitious data that appears legitimate.
In the legal and procurement sectors, AI hallucinations have led to severe court sanctions. In Oregon, for example, an attorney was fined $10,000 for submitting a brief containing fifteen fabricated legal citations generated by an AI assistant.
If the Implore codebase generates inaccurate procurement bids, quotes false regulatory compliance metrics, or includes non-existent legal authorities in its outputs, users relying on those outputs can face disqualification, loss of government contracts, or severe financial damages.
From a liability standpoint, the legal system divides software failures into products and services:
 * **Product Liability:** Under the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC), mass-produced, off-the-shelf software is classified as a "good," subjecting the developer to strict product liability standards for design defects or warning failures.
 * **Service Negligence:** Conversely, highly customized software specifically designed for an individual client is classified as a "service," subjecting the developer to a standard negligence framework where the plaintiff must prove a breach of reasonable care.
 * **The Section 230 Limitation:** Developers cannot rely on Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act to shield themselves from liability for AI-generated outputs. While Section 230 protects online platforms from liability for third-party user-generated content, it does not immunize a platform when it acts as an active publisher or creator of its own content. Because generative AI systems create novel content rather than passively hosting third-party data, the developer is legally classified as the author of the output and faces full liability for any resulting harm.
### Legal Wrappers, Disclaimers, and Contractual Indemnification
To manage these liabilities, developers must implement a robust "legal wrapper" around the Implore codebase. This involves structuring contractual terms, disclaimers, and indemnification provisions to allocate risk away from the development entity.
The essential components of this liability mitigation framework are structured in the table below:
| Risk Category | Technical Exposure | Legal Precedent / Statute | Mitigation Protocol |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unauthorized Practice of Law** | Automated drafting of contract terms and regulatory analyses  | OK Stat. Tit. 5; Rule 5.5 (UPL restriction) | Mandatory "Human-in-the-Loop" (attorney) review before submission |
| **Generative AI Hallucinations** | Fictitious citations, incorrect FAR guidelines, or false data  | Professional Negligence / Tort Law  | Strict disclaimers of accuracy; conspicuous warning labels on all outputs |
| **Section 230 Exposure** | AI-generated recommendations causing financial or contractual losses | 47 U.S.C. § 230 (Loss of immunity for active publishers) | Absolute exclusion of consequential damages in End User License Agreements (EULA) |
| **Third-Party Data Breaches** | Ingestion of sensitive proprietary or classified government data  | HIPAA, GDPR, CCPA, CPRA, Data Privacy Act  | Strict bilateral indemnification clauses in vendor and client agreements  |
| **Product Defect Claims** | Algorithmic errors leading to defective procurement submissions  | Strict Product Liability / UCC | Classification of the software as a service rather than a good; limit remedies to fee refunds |
Bilateral indemnification provisions are highly effective tools for managing these risks. These clauses legally bind the user to compensate the developer for any losses, legal fees, or damages arising from the misuse of the software, regulatory non-compliance, or intellectual property disputes.
By establishing these risk-allocation parameters on the front end, the development entity secures essential financial protection and holds users accountable for validating AI-generated outputs before they are formally submitted to the federal government.
